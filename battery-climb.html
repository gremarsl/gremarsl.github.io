How high can 500 g fly on 1 kWh?

A cube, a drone, a ball and a drop you can tune. Each weighs 500 g, has two propellers and a 1 kWh battery, and starts at sea level. The motors draw constant power until the battery is empty.

500 W

Power draw is the electrical power the motors take from the battery, in watts. One watt is one joule of energy per second. It is not a force. At 500 W the 1 kWh battery is empty after 2 hours.

What the model says

  • Below 87 W the cube, drone and ball do not lift off. That is the power needed to hover at sea level.
  • Just above that, the object rises slowly and then hangs in the air for hours. Almost all of the battery goes into holding position.
  • More power means more height. Thinner air needs more power to hover, so the object stops where its power is only enough to hover. Height is set by power, and the energy is rarely the limit: 1 kWh could lift 500 g about 730 km if nothing were lost.
  • Too much power empties the battery before the object reaches that ceiling. The best setting here is about 10 kW.
  • Shape only changes drag. Up to the best power, objects with the same propellers reach the same height. Low drag gets there sooner, and at very high power it also gets higher.
  • Propeller size changes much more. Give the drop 25 cm propellers and it climbs about 9 km higher at the same power.

Why the ball and the drop climb faster

The four objects differ only in drag. Drag is a force, measured in newtons. It is the push of the air against the motion, so on a climbing object it points down, like the weight.

Air presses on every part of the surface. Air that hits the front is slowed down and presses harder than normal. Where the air cannot follow the surface, it breaks away and leaves a wake of swirling air behind the body, which presses less than normal. The front is pushed back harder than the back is pushed forward, and the difference is the drag. A smaller part comes from air rubbing along the surface.

The first part alone, ½ρv², is a pressure: kg/(m·s²) = N/m² = Pa. Pressure times area gives a force.

Example at 50 m/s and sea level: stopped air pushes with ½ · 1.225 · 50² = 1531 Pa. The cube has Cd = 1.05 and a front of 0.01 m², so its drag is 1531 · 1.05 · 0.01 = 16.1 N. That is more than three times its weight of 4.9 N. The drop has Cd = 0.05 and a front of 0.0079 m², so its drag is 0.6 N.

  • Cube and drone. The air breaks away at the sharp front edges. The wake is wider than the body, so the whole back sits in low pressure. The flat drone body is also the widest.
  • Ball. The air follows the round front and breaks away later. The front is pushed less and the wake is narrower.
  • Drop. The long tail fills the space where the wake would be. The air stays attached and the pressure recovers behind the body. Almost only skin friction is left.
  • Speed and altitude. Drag grows with the square of the speed and shrinks with the air density. Set the altitude to 30 km and the drag almost disappears. Shape decides how fast an object gets through the dense lower air, but not where its ceiling is.
  • Why the cube still gets as high as the drop. At its highest point an object no longer climbs. Its speed is zero, so its drag is zero, whatever the shape. What is left is hovering, and that depends only on weight, propellers, air density and power. Drag only costs time on the way up. At 500 W the cube needs 20 minutes and the drop 15, and the battery lasts 2 hours. The drop only gets higher when the battery runs out during the climb, at around 10 kW and more.

The physics

1. Energy is not the limit

The battery holds E = 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ. Lifting a mass m to a height h stores the energy m g h. If the whole battery went into height:

h=Emg=3 600 000 J0.5 kg⋅9.81 m/s²≈734 km

The simulation reaches only a few percent of that. A propeller has to pay for staying in the air every second, even when it gains no height.

2. Why hovering costs power

A propeller makes thrust by pushing air downward. The thrust T equals the momentum it gives to the air each second. Momentum theory treats the propellers as a disk of area A. Air passes through the disk at the speed vi and ends up at twice that speed, w = 2vi, further below. With the air density ρ, the air mass per second and the thrust are:

m˙=ρAvi,T=m˙w=2ρAvi2

The power is the kinetic energy given to that air each second:

P=12m˙w2=Tvi

To hover, the thrust must equal the weight, T = m g. That fixes the air speed and the power:

vi=mg2ρA,Phover=(mg)3/22ρA

Thrust grows with the air speed, but power grows with its square. Moving a lot of air slowly is cheaper than moving a little air fast, so large propellers are efficient.

Example: two 12.7 cm propellers have A = 0.0253 m². At sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³) the air passes through them at 8.9 m/s and hovering puts 43.6 W into the air. At 50 % efficiency that is 87 W from the battery.

3. Climbing

When the object climbs at the speed v, air passes through the disk at v + vi:

T=2ρA(v+vi)vi,Pair=T(v+vi)

The part T v is useful work against gravity and drag. The part T vi is lost in the downwash. Eliminating vi gives the equation the simulation solves for v. Here P is the power drawn from the battery and η the efficiency from battery to air:

ηP=T(v2+v24+T2ρA)

In a steady climb the thrust carries the weight plus the drag:

T=mg+12ρCdAfv2

Af is the frontal area of the body and Cd its drag coefficient. Drag grows with the square of the speed, so it only matters in a fast climb.

4. Thinner air

Air density falls by about half every 5 km. As a rule of thumb:

ρ(h)≈ρ0e−h/H,H≈7.5 km

The simulation uses the International Standard Atmosphere, which also follows the temperature layers. Thin air means less drag, but also less air to push against. Hover power grows with 1/√ρ.

5. The ceiling

The object stops climbing at the density ρc where all of its power is needed to hover:

ηP=(mg)3/22ρcA⇒ρc=ρ0(P0P)2

P0 is the battery power needed to hover at sea level. With the rule of thumb for the density, the ceiling height is:

hc≈2HlnPP0

Example: 500 W gives 2 · 7.5 km · ln(500 / 87) ≈ 26 km. The simulation says 25.6 km. Every doubling of power adds roughly 9 to 10 km. The shape does not appear in this formula. That is why objects with the same propellers reach the same height.

6. The battery sets the time

t=EP

At 500 W the battery lasts 2 hours, at 10 kW only 6 minutes. If the climb takes longer than that, the object never reaches its ceiling. This is the falling right side of the chart. The best power is the one where the battery runs empty just as the object arrives at its ceiling. Below it, energy is wasted hovering at the top. Above it, the ceiling is out of reach, and a low-drag shape helps because it climbs faster.

7. Where the energy goes

The table column "Battery turned into height" is the first term divided by E:

ηh=mghE

At 500 W it is about 3.5 %. Half of the battery is lost in the motors and propellers. Almost all of the rest goes into downwash while the object hovers at the top.

8. How to improve it

  • Larger propellers. Hover power falls with 1/diameter. Doubling the diameter halves it and lifts the ceiling by about 9 km. Try 25 cm for the drop.
  • Better efficiency η. It raises the ceiling like extra power, without draining the battery faster.
  • Less mass. Hover power grows with m3/2, so 20 % less mass needs about 28 % less power.
  • The right power. Choose it so the battery is empty when the object arrives at the top. Time spent hovering buys no height.
  • Less drag. It pays off only near and above the best power. Compare the drop with the cube at 10 kW, then at 500 W.
  • Wings. Not modelled here. A wing moves far more air than a small propeller, so a winged climb needs much less power for the same weight.

Limits of the model

  • Ideal propellers (momentum theory) scaled by the efficiency above. Standard atmosphere. Gravity weakens with altitude.
  • Drag: cube with 10 cm edges, drag coefficient 1.05. Ball with 12.4 cm diameter, 0.47. Drone with a flat 15 × 10 cm body, 1.2. Drop as set above.
  • Climb speed is capped at 250 m/s, because propellers stop working near the speed of sound.
  • When the battery is empty the object coasts upward a little, then falls.
  • Not modelled: a real 1 kWh battery weighs about 4 kg, and a real fixed propeller of this size cannot spin fast enough to hold 500 g above roughly 10 to 15 km.
